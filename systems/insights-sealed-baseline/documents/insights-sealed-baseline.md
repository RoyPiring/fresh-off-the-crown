## Reaching an Evidence-Based Routine Verdict

### Measuring the report against a sealed baseline

I measured the overlap between the Claude Code /insights report and the friction list I had written from memory before generating the report. The timestamped list created a fixed baseline that the later findings could not change.

I compared each report row with one frozen item and classified it as Confirmed or New. I also counted each sealed item that the report did not raise. This produced traceable measurements instead of a general feeling that the report looked accurate or useful.

The first comparison produced 4 confirmed report rows, 5 new rows, and 6 unraised sealed items. A later close-match audit corrected those results to 3 confirmed, 6 new, and 7 unraised. The corrected counts supported WATCH. The report found useful issues that memory missed, but it did not capture enough known friction to replace my own review process.

### Tracing all four measurements to source evidence

Measurement 1 came directly from the report header captured at 23:46:42: 1,745 messages across 71 sessions, with 179 total. I copied those values as written and did not derive or estimate them.

Measurements 2 and 3 came from nine transcribed report rows. Each row preserved enough wording to find it again in report-2026-08-31-234511.html and included either a named frozen-list match or a reason no match existed.

Measurement 4 came from the ten-item list sealed at 23:37:36. The first comparison counted 4 confirmed rows, 5 new rows, and 6 unraised items. The checks 4+5=9 and 4+6=10 reconciled both sources. The weak R5-to-F9 match remained visible in its row. A later audit rejected that match and corrected the totals to 3 confirmed, 6 new, and 7 unraised.

### The evidence that shaped my decision

The unraised sealed items carried the most weight. The initial comparison showed that the report omitted 6 of 10 frictions I had already recorded. The close-match audit later increased that count to 7, leaving only 3 confirmed findings. That was too much missing evidence for ADOPT.

The new findings kept the report out of PARK. The initial review found 5 new rows, including authentication expiry and the volume of buggy code. After the ambiguous match was corrected, the new count became 6. These were issues my memory-based list had not captured.

The difference between 49 and 71 sessions served as a tiebreaker, not the main reason for the verdict. It did not alter the recorded row counts, but it left the friction denominator unestablished. Together, the missed known issues, useful new findings, and uncertain denominator supported WATCH.

## Sealing a Pre-Run Decision Record

### Freezing the method before reviewing evidence

I wrote the memory-based friction list and decision method before generating the Claude Code /insights report. The list contained ten numbered items and was sealed with a timestamp of 23:37:36.

The method required a one-to-one comparison between each report-named friction and the frozen list. Every report row had to be marked Confirmed or New, while every sealed item not matched by the report had to be counted as unraised.

I also defined how the final verdict would use four measurements rather than a general reaction to the report. Freezing the list prevented the report from changing the baseline, while freezing the method prevented the outcome from being justified with new rules after the evidence appeared. The record contained no predicted verdict. ADOPT, WATCH, or PARK had to follow from the comparison and declared triggers.

### Protecting the verdict from hindsight bias

The timestamped seal fixed the friction list at 10 items at 23:37:36. After that moment, I could not add an item because /insights mentioned it or remove one because the report failed to recognize it.

This made overlap between the sources meaningful. A Confirmed row had to connect report wording with an item that existed before the report file. A New row required an explicit reason why no frozen item matched. Unraised items remained visible instead of disappearing from the evaluation.

The evidence rule allowed measurements only from the frozen list or the report. The decision record also described the comparison before either outcome was known. This prevented a useful-looking report from defining its own success criteria after the fact. The seal did not guarantee perfect classification, but it preserved the original baseline so later close-match decisions could be audited without rewriting history.

## Turning First Impressions Into Traceable Findings

### Preserving each report-named friction

I transcribed each distinct friction named in the Claude Code report into a structured nine-row list. Each row retained enough of the report’s language to be found again in report-2026-08-31-234511.html.

I then compared every row with the ten-item sealed baseline. A row received a named frozen item when the wording supported a match. If no item fit, I marked the row New and wrote why the frozen list did not cover it.

This structure allowed the confirmed and new counts to be recalculated without trusting the totals I wrote. It also exposed the close R5-to-F9 classification for later review. The first pass counted 4 Confirmed and 5 New rows, leaving 6 sealed items unraised. After auditing the ambiguous match, I corrected the result to 3 Confirmed, 6 New, and 7 unraised while preserving the original reasoning.

### Why recall alone cannot determine the verdict

My first impression said the report felt useful because its frictions looked recognizable. That statement cited no row, count, or named item from either source, so another reviewer could not reproduce or challenge it.

The reaction also came after I had read the report. Its wording and categories had already influenced what felt familiar, creating the hindsight problem the 23:37:36 seal was meant to control.

The committed method required a one-to-one comparison, four recorded measurements, and a verdict tied to declared triggers. Recognition could suggest that a match existed, but it could not prove one. The row-level audit later showed why this distinction mattered. One apparently familiar report category had been matched too generously with a narrower frozen item. A structured comparison caught that error, while the original feeling would have counted it without exposing the mismatch.

## Auditing the Most Ambiguous Match

### Adjudicating a close classification without changing the sealed list

The close-match audit rejected the link between R5, “Wrong Approach / 30,” and F9, “Choosing the wrong tool for a job, shell where a dedicated tool fits, or the reverse.” The frozen item described a specific cause, while the report row named a broader outcome category.

I moved R5 from Confirmed to New and returned F9 to the unraised list. This changed the totals from 4 confirmed, 5 new, and 6 unraised to 3 confirmed, 6 new, and 7 unraised.

The correction made the report’s coverage weaker. It confirmed fewer than one-third of the frictions I had remembered, and ADOPT moved further away. The verdict still remained WATCH because 3 confirmed findings stayed above the PARK trigger of 2 or fewer, while the 6 new findings preserved independent value. The audit did not reverse the decision, but it exposed that my first classification had been too generous.

## Reflecting on the Audit and Next Steps

### Tools and evidence-based evaluation concepts

I used the Claude Code CLI to generate the /insights report, the local Windows terminal to verify the existing installation, and a structured worksheet to preserve the audit trail.

The main method was to freeze the baseline before reviewing tool output. I then classified each report friction against one sealed item and recorded confirmed, new, and unraised counts. Timestamps, source wording, and reconciliation checks made those measurements traceable.

I also learned that close matches require separate review. A broad report category does not automatically confirm a narrower remembered cause. Rechecking R5 against F9 changed the final counts to 3 confirmed, 6 new, and 7 unraised. The verdict remained WATCH because the report added evidence I had missed but failed to capture most known friction. Specific reversal triggers kept that judgment open to later reports rather than treating one run as permanent.

### Measured project time and key challenge

This build took approximately 45 minutes. The hardest part was auditing the closest match between the report rows and my frozen friction list without changing the baseline after seeing the findings.

R5 initially appeared to confirm F9 because both involved choosing a wrong approach. A closer reading showed that F9 named a specific tool-choice cause, while R5 described a broader report category. Preserving that distinction required moving R5 to New and returning F9 to unraised.

That correction changed the measurements from 4/5/6 to 3 confirmed, 6 new, and 7 unraised. It did not change the WATCH verdict, but it reduced the evidence supporting the report’s coverage. The challenge showed that an audit can follow the right process and still contain a generous classification. Row-level wording made the error visible and correctable without altering the sealed list.

### Applying the learning to future work

I completed this build to learn how to audit AI-generated insights against a baseline created before the report existed. The process replaced first impressions with timestamped, row-level evidence and a verdict tied to declared rules.

The final comparison found 3 confirmed report rows, 6 new rows, and 7 sealed frictions the report did not raise. That mix supported WATCH. The tool surfaced useful issues outside my memory, but it did not cover enough known friction to replace my own review.

I want to apply the same forensic documentation method to other developer-focused AI tools. Future audits should preserve the original prediction, generated artifact, evidence order, row classifications, corrected counts, and reversal triggers. This will let me compare what a tool claims, what it detects, what it misses, and whether repeated use earns a place in my daily workflow.
